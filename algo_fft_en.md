## Conventions

- All fields considered have characteristic 2: $-$ and $+$ coincide, and only $+$ is written below.
- $X$ is the indeterminate of polynomials. A polynomial is also seen as a function: $f(a)$ is the value of $f$ at $a$.
- "DFT" is used for the evaluation of a polynomial on all the points of a set (here: a coset of an $\mathbb{F}_2$-vector space of $\mathbb{F}$, not a set of roots of unity). Given a polynomial $f$ and a set $S$, the DFT of $f$ on $S$ is the list of the $f(a)$, $a\in S$.

## Von zur Gathen - Gerhard, Cantor - Wang - Zhu

### Von zur Gathen - Gerhard

Notations:

- $\mathbb{F}$ is the finite field of size $2^n$,
- $\{\beta_1, \ldots, \beta_n\}$ is a basis of $\mathbb{F}$ over $\mathbb{F}_2$,
- $W_0=\{0\}$, $W_i=\langle\beta_1, \ldots, \beta_i\rangle$ is the $\mathbb{F}_2$-vector space generated by $\beta_1, \ldots, \beta_i$ (it has $2^i$ elements), and $C_i=\langle\beta_{i+1}, \ldots, \beta_n\rangle$, $i=1\ldots n$. We have $\mathbb{F} = W_i \oplus C_i$,
- $\sigma_i$ is the polynomial that cancels exactly on $W_i$:
$$\sigma_i = \prod_{a\in W_i} (X+a),$$
so that $\deg \sigma_i = 2^i$.

We have $\sigma_0(X) = X$ and, since $W_{i+1} = W_i \cup (W_i + \beta_{i+1})$ (disjoint union),

$$\sigma_{i+1}(X) = \sigma_i(X) \: \sigma_i(X+\beta_{i+1}). \quad (R_0)$$

By induction, each $\sigma_i$ is a *linearized polynomial*: it only has monomials whose degree is a power of 2, $\sigma_i = \sum_{d\leq i} s_{i,d} X^{2^d}$. The function $a \mapsto \sigma_i(a)$ is therefore $\mathbb{F}_2$-linear, and $\sigma_i(X + a) = \sigma_i(X) + \sigma_i(a)$ for all $a$. Applying this to $(R_0)$ gives

$$\sigma_{i+1}(X) = \sigma_i(X)^2 + \sigma_i(\beta_{i+1}) \: \sigma_i(X). \quad (R)$$

(Squaring a linearized polynomial gives a linearized polynomial, so $\sigma_{i+1}$ is linearized too, which proves the induction.)

$\sigma_i$ has at most $i+1$ non-zero terms (the monomials $X^{2^d}$, $d\leq i$), therefore, for all $a$, $\sigma_i(X+a) = \sigma_i(X) + \sigma_i(a)$ has at most $i+2$ non-zero terms: the Euclidean division by such a polynomial is cheap.

This suffices to deduce the Von zur Gathen - Gerhard DFT algorithm in $\mathbb{F}$. Let $\varepsilon \in C_{i+1}$: the coset $W_{i+1}+\varepsilon$ is the disjoint union of $W_i+\varepsilon$ and $W_i+\varepsilon+\beta_{i+1}$. Let $g$ be a polynomial of degree $<2^{i+1}$ (typically $g = f \bmod \sigma_{i+1}(X+\varepsilon)$ for a given polynomial $f$: $g$ and $f$ have the same values on $W_{i+1}+\varepsilon$). We want to compute

- $g_0 = g \bmod \sigma_i(X+\varepsilon)$, which has the same values as $g$ on $W_i+\varepsilon$,
- $g_1 = g \bmod \sigma_i(X+\varepsilon+\beta_{i+1})$, which has the same values as $g$ on $W_i+\varepsilon+\beta_{i+1}$.

Let $q$ be the quotient of the division of $g$ by $\sigma_i(X+\varepsilon)$, so that $g = g_0 + \sigma_i(X+\varepsilon) \, q$, $\deg g_0 < 2^i$ and $\deg q < 2^i$. By linearity, $\sigma_i(X+\varepsilon) = \sigma_i(X+\varepsilon+\beta_{i+1}) + \sigma_i(\beta_{i+1})$, hence
$$g = \left[g_0 + \sigma_i(\beta_{i+1}) \, q\right] + \sigma_i(X+\varepsilon+\beta_{i+1}) \, q.$$
The term between brackets has degree $<2^i$, therefore
$$g_1 = g_0 + \sigma_i(\beta_{i+1}) \, q.$$

Starting from $g$, we can thus compute $g_0$ and $g_1$ for the price of a **single Euclidean division** (which gives $g_0$ and $q$) followed by the addition of $q \times \text{constant}$ to $g_0$. This is the core algorithmic trick that gives the complexity of the Von zur Gathen - Gerhard algorithm.

The algorithm starts from a polynomial $f$ of degree $<2^n$ and applies this computation for $i=n-1,\ldots,0$. At step $i$, $2^{n-1-i}$ Euclidean divisions are performed, one for each coset $W_{i+1}+\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon \in C_{i+1}$ (at the first step, $i=n-1$, $g=f$ and there is a single division). At the end of the last step ($i=0$), the polynomials obtained are the remainders $f \bmod (X+a)$, that is, the values $f(a)$ for all $a\in\mathbb{F}$.

The algorithm performs $O(k \log^2(k))$ additions or multiplications, $k=2^n$. See [1] p.99 for a precise analysis of the number of additions and multiplications performed in $\mathbb{F}$.

### Improvement: Cantor - Wang - Zhu, Cantor bases

The Von zur Gathen - Gerhard algorithm does not make any assumption about the basis $\{\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_n\}$ used. Choosing the right basis reduces the number of multiplications to be performed, by making sure that all the non-zero coefficients of the $\sigma_i$ are equal to 1, and that $\sigma_i(\beta_{i+1}) = 1$ for any $i$. For this, we choose the $\beta_i$ such that
$$\beta_1 = 1 \text{ and } \forall i < n,\ \beta_i = \beta_{i+1}^2 + \beta_{i+1}. \quad (E)$$

The basis obtained is a *Cantor basis*. Such a basis exists only if $n$ is a power of two; let's write $n=2^s$. See below for a justification of this and for the construction of the basis.

Let $\varphi(X) = X^2 + X$. $(E)$ says that $\varphi(\beta_{i+1}) = \beta_i$ for $i<n$, and $\varphi(\beta_1) = \varphi(1) = 0$. With a Cantor basis, it is shown by induction from $(R)$ that

$$\forall i,\ \sigma_i = \varphi^{\circ i} \text{ and } \sigma_i(\beta_{i+1}) = 1.$$

Indeed, $\sigma_0 = X$; if $\sigma_i = \varphi^{\circ i}$, then $\sigma_i(\beta_{i+1}) = \varphi^{\circ i}(\beta_{i+1}) = \beta_1 = 1$ by $(E)$, so $(R)$ becomes $\sigma_{i+1} = \sigma_i^2 + \sigma_i = \varphi \circ \sigma_i = \varphi^{\circ (i+1)}$.

In particular all the non-zero coefficients of the $\sigma_i$ are equal to 1. This decreases the number of multiplications to be performed during the Euclidean divisions in the Von zur Gathen - Gerhard algorithm. The number of multiplications to evaluate a polynomial of degree $k$ is in $O(k \log(k))$ and the number of additions becomes $O(k \log^{1.585}(k))$. See [1] p.106 for the calculation of these bounds.

The improved algorithm is called the **Cantor** or **Wang-Zhu** algorithm.

Additional properties of Cantor bases are useful for the Mateer-Gao algorithm presented in the next section.

- For $t=2^j \leq n$, $\sigma_{t}=X^{2^t} + X$. This is shown by induction:
  - $\sigma_1=X^2+X$;
  - if $\sigma_t = \varphi^{\circ t} = X^{2^t} + X$, then, as $\sigma_t$ is linear,
$\sigma_{2t} = \varphi^{\circ 2t} = \sigma_t \circ \sigma_t = (X^{2^t} + X)^{2^t} + X^{2^t} + X = X^{2^{2t}} + X^{2^t} + X^{2^t} + X = X^{2^{2t}}+X$.
- For an integer $j = \sum_{i = 0}^{n-1} \varepsilon_i \, 2^i$, $\varepsilon_i \in \{0,1\}$, let
$$\omega_j = \sum_{i = 0}^{n-1} \varepsilon_i \beta_{i+1}.$$
  (So $\omega_j$ is the element of $\mathbb{F}$ whose coordinates in the basis $\{\beta_i\}$ are the bits of $j$; in particular $\omega_0 = 0$, $\omega_1 = 1$ and $W_i = \{\omega_j, j < 2^i\}$.) Then
  $$\sigma_i(\omega_j) = \omega_{j\gg i}$$
  where $\gg$ represents the right shift (i.e. $j \gg i = \lfloor j/2^i \rfloor$). This follows from
  - the linearity of $\sigma_i = \varphi^{\circ i}$,
  - the fact that $\varphi(\beta_k) = \beta_{k-1}$ for $k>1$, and $\varphi(\beta_1) = 0$: $\sigma_i(\beta_k) = \beta_{k-i}$ if $k > i$ and $0$ otherwise.

As a particular case, $\sigma_i(\beta_{i+1})=\sigma_i(\omega_{2^{i}})=\omega_{1} = 1$.

Let us also mention the following formula (not used in the following):
$$\sigma_i (X) = \sum_{d=0}^{i} \binom{i}{d} \ X^{2^d}$$
The $\binom{i}{d}$ are the binomial coefficients, understood modulo 2. The formula follows from the binomial theorem: $\varphi = F + \mathrm{Id}$ where $F: a\mapsto a^2$ is the Frobenius map, and $F$ and $\mathrm{Id}$ commute, so $\sigma_i = \varphi^{\circ i} = (F+\mathrm{Id})^{i} = \sum_d \binom{i}{d} F^{d}$.

#### Construction of a Cantor basis

To build such a basis, we can proceed in two ways:

#### "Inner" construction

Starting from a given field $\mathbb{F}$ of cardinal $2^{n}$, $n=2^s$, let us notice that:

- $\sigma_n(X) = X^{2^n} + X$ (see above, $n$ is a power of 2) has exactly the $2^n$ elements of $\mathbb{F}$ as roots;
- $\sigma_n = \sigma_{n-i} \circ \sigma_i$; since $\sigma_{n-i}(0)=0$, the roots of $\sigma_i$ are also roots of $\sigma_n$, so they are all in $\mathbb{F}$.

We choose $\beta_n \in \mathbb{F}$ such that $\sigma_{n-1}(\beta_n) \neq 0$, and for all $i < n$, $\beta_i = \varphi(\beta_{i+1})$. Then $\varphi(\beta_1) = \sigma_n(\beta_n) = 0$ (as $\beta_n \in \mathbb{F}$) but $\beta_1 = \sigma_{n-1}(\beta_n) \neq 0$: as $\varphi(X)=0$ has roots 0 and 1, $\beta_1=1$.

As $\sigma_{n-1}$ has $2^{n-1}$ roots in $\mathbb{F}$, there are $2^{n-1}$ valid choices for $\beta_n$, that is to say, half of the values of $\mathbb{F}$; it is thus not difficult to find a valid value for $\beta_n$.

Starting from a constructed basis $\{\beta_i\}$, and with $\beta$ a linear combination of $\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_{n-1}$, let $\gamma_n = \beta_n + \beta$ and for all $i < n$, $\gamma_i = \varphi(\gamma_{i+1})$. Then by linearity of $\sigma_n$ and $\sigma_{n-1}$, $\sigma_n(\gamma_n)=0$ and $\sigma_{n-1}(\gamma_n) = \sigma_{n-1}(\beta_n) + \sigma_{n-1}(\beta) = \sigma_{n-1}(\beta_n) \neq 0$ (as $\beta \in W_{n-1}$ is a root of $\sigma_{n-1}$): $\{\gamma_i\}$ is another Cantor basis. All Cantor bases are obtained in this way because there are $2^{n-1}$ possible values for $\beta$, which is the number of valid choices for $\beta_n$.

This construction shows that whenever $n=2^s$, a Cantor basis exists.

#### "Outer" construction: joint construction of the field and the basis

We can also construct $\mathbb{F}$ jointly with the $\beta_i$, by successive extensions of $\mathbb{F}_{2}$: if $\beta_1, \beta_2, \ldots,\beta_i$ are already constructed with $\beta_1=1$, $\beta_{i+1}$ is constructed as a solution of $(E)$, i.e. of $X^2 + X = \beta_i$ (the field generated by $\beta_1, \ldots,\beta_i$ is a subfield of $\mathbb{F}$ of size a power of 2). If a field of dimension $n={2^s}$ over $\mathbb{F}_2$ has been produced thus far, this construction works for $\beta_1, \ldots, \beta_n$ (see the discussion for the "inner" construction). When there is no solution in the field $\langle\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_i\rangle$ generated so far, the field is extended by an element *defined* by $(E)$ (a root of $X^2 + X + \beta_i$, which is irreducible), which has the effect of doubling the dimension of the field over $\mathbb{F}_2$.

A related argument shows why, when $\mathbb{F}$ has a Cantor basis, one must have $n=2^s$: if $\beta_1, \ldots, \beta_n$ is a Cantor basis, then, for each $i$, either $\beta_{i+1}$ belongs to $k=\mathbb{F}_2[\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_{i}]$, or $k[\beta_{i+1}]$ is an extension of degree 2 of $k$ ($\beta_{i+1}$ is a root of $X^2 + X + \beta_i \in k[X]$). In the end, $\mathbb{F}$ is a tower of extensions of degree 2 of $\mathbb{F}_2$, therefore has degree $2^s$ over it for some $s$.

## Mateer-Gao

The algorithm is described in [2] and in [1].

### Idea

A Cantor basis is used, with the same notations as above.

Input:
- $s \geq 0$, which fixes the size of the DFT: $\eta = 2^{2^s}$ (for $s\geq1$ we also define $t = 2^{s-1}$, $\tau = 2^t$, so that $\eta = 2^{2t} = \tau^2$),
- $f$ a polynomial of degree $< \eta$,
- an *offset* $j < 2^{n}/\eta$.

Output: in an array of size $\eta$, the values of $f$ on $W_{2^s} + \omega_{\eta j}$, in this order: $\{f(\omega_{\eta j}), f(\omega_{\eta j + 1}), \ldots, f(\omega_{\eta j + \eta - 1})\}$. (If $s \geq 1$, $W_{2^s} = W_{2t}$.)

In the following, $s \geq 1$. We have

$$\eta = \tau^2,$$
$$\sigma_t(X) = X^{2^t} + X = X^{\tau} + X,$$
$$\sigma_{2t}(X) = X^{2^{2t}} + X = X^{\eta} + X.$$

If $f$ is processed as part of a higher level recursive call, $f$ represents the initial polynomial $f_0$ modulo $\sigma_{2t}(X+ \omega_{\eta j})$ (the polynomial that cancels on $W_{2t} + \omega_{\eta j}$). $f$ and $f_0$ coincide on $W_{2t} + \omega_{\eta j}$.

The $\sigma_{k}$ are linear and as shown previously,

$$\sigma_{k}(\omega_{j}) = \omega_{j\gg k}.$$

$\sigma_{2t}$ cancels on $W_{2t}$ and $\sigma_{t}$ on $W_t$; the cosets $W_t+\omega_{\tau k}$, $k < \tau$, partition $W_{2t}$, so
$$\sigma_{2t}(X) = \prod_{k < \tau} \sigma_{t}(X + \omega_{\tau k}) = \prod_{k < \tau} \left [\sigma_{t}(X) + \omega_{k} \right ],$$
since $\sigma_t(\omega_{\tau k}) = \omega_{(\tau k) \gg t} = \omega_k$.

This relation can be translated by $\omega_{\eta j}$ to give
$$\sigma_{2t}(X + \omega_{\eta j}) = \sigma_{2t}(X) + \omega_j = \prod_{k < \tau} \left[\sigma_{t}(X + \omega_{\eta j}) + \omega_{k} \right] = \prod_{k < \tau} \left[\sigma_{t}(X) + \omega_{\tau j} + \omega_{k} \right] = \prod_{k < \tau} \left[\sigma_{t}(X) + \omega_{\tau j + k} \right],$$
using $\sigma_{2t}(\omega_{\eta j}) = \omega_{j}$, $\sigma_{t}(\omega_{\eta j}) = \omega_{\tau j}$, and the fact that $\tau j$ and $k < \tau$ have no bit in common.

Let $R_k = \sigma_{t}(X) + \omega_{\tau j + k} = X^{\tau} + X + \omega_{\tau j + k}$, $k < \tau$. The $R_k$ are the factors in the product above; $R_k$ cancels exactly on $W_t + \omega_{\eta j + \tau k}$ (indeed $\sigma_t(\omega_{\eta j + \tau k}) = \omega_{\tau j + k}$), and these cosets partition $W_{2t} + \omega_{\eta j}$.

If we deduce from $f = f_0 \bmod \sigma_{2t}(X + \omega_{\eta j})$ the set of the $f_0 \bmod R_k$, $k < \tau$, it is then possible to finish the evaluation of $f_0$ on $W_{2t} + \omega_{\eta j}$ by performing a DFT on each of the $f_0 \bmod R_k$, which will give the values of $f_0$ on the spaces $W_t + \omega_{\eta j + \tau k}$, $k< \tau$, which partition $W_{2t} + \omega_{\eta j}$.

**The efficiency of the algorithm comes from not doing the computations of $f_0$ mod $R_k = \sigma_{t}(X) + \omega_{\tau j + k}$ for $k < \tau$ independently**. Instead, we proceed as follows:

First, $f$ is decomposed as

$$f(X) = \sum_{\ell<\tau} f_{\ell}(X) \: \sigma_{t}(X)^{\ell}$$
with $\forall \ell < \tau, \deg f_{\ell} < \tau$.

(This is a "generalized Taylor decomposition", which only requires additions; see below.)

Then, as $R_k = \sigma_{t} + \omega_{\tau j + k}$, $\sigma_t^\ell \equiv \omega_{\tau j + k}^{\ell} \bmod R_k$ and

$$f \bmod R_k = \sum_{\ell<\tau} f_{\ell} \: \omega_{\tau j + k}^{\ell}.$$

In other words, the $\{f \bmod R_k, k < \tau\}$ are evaluations of the polynomial $P(Y)= \sum_{\ell<\tau} f_\ell(X) Y^\ell$ on the points $\omega_{\tau j + k}$, $k<\tau$, of the space $W_t + \omega_{\tau j}$. These evaluations can therefore be performed by a DFT. The fact that the coefficients of $P$ and the values to compute are themselves polynomials (in $X$, of degree $<\tau$) is not a problem: $\tau$ DFTs are performed, one for each degree in $X$, and the final values are obtained by reassembling the DFT results.

The recursion step ends with a DFT of each of the $f \bmod R_k$, which produces the desired values of $f$ (or $f_0$).

This gives in practice the following algorithm:

### Pseudo-algorithm

If $\eta = 2$ ($s = 0$), a direct computation is performed: for $f = f_{0} + f_{1} X$, $u = f(\omega_{2j}) = f_{0} + f_{1}\,\omega_{2j}$ and $f(\omega_{2j+1}) = f_{0} + f_{1} (\omega_{2j} + 1) = u + f_{1}$.

Otherwise, $s > 0$. The following steps are followed:

1) Taylor decomposition of $f$ w.r.t. $X^{\tau} + X$, $\tau = 2^t = \sqrt{\eta}$ (computed with additions only, see the section "Taylor decomposition" below):
$f(X) = \sum_{i<\tau} f_i(X) (X^{\tau} + X)^i$ with $\deg f_i < \tau$, that is
$f(X) = g(X, X^{\tau}+X)$ with
$$g(X,Y) = \sum_{i < \tau} f_i(X) Y^i = \sum_{i < \tau, k < \tau} f_{i,k} \ X^k Y^i,$$
$f_{i,k} =$ degree-$k$ coefficient of $f_i$. The coefficients of $f_i$ are written in the rows of a square matrix $M$ (row $i$ = $f_i$, so the coefficient at position $(i,k)$ is $f_{i,k}$).

2) Computation of $\tau$ DFTs of size $\tau$ on the columns of $M$, to obtain the values of the corresponding polynomials on $W_t + \omega_{\tau j}$; i.e. $\tau$ recursive calls with parameters $s - 1$, the coefficients of the column, and offset $j$.
Before the DFT, the column $k$ contains the polynomial in $Y$
$$P_k = \sum_{i < \tau} f_{i,k} \ Y^i.$$
After the DFT, the coefficient $(i,k)$ is
$$\sum_{\ell < \tau} f_{\ell,k} \ \omega_{\tau j + i}^{\ell}.$$
Row $i$ therefore represents the polynomial
$$\sum_{k<\tau} \left (\sum_{\ell < \tau} f_{\ell,k} \: \omega_{\tau j + i}^\ell \right) X^k =
\sum_{\ell < \tau} f_{\ell}(X) \ \omega_{\tau j + i}^{\ell},$$
that is, $f \bmod R_i$.

3) Computation of $\tau$ DFTs of size $\tau$ on the rows of the matrix, i.e. for all $i < \tau$, the DFT of $f \bmod R_i$ on $W_t + \omega_{\eta j + \tau i}$.
On row $i$, we thus recursively call the algorithm with the coefficients of the row, the parameter $s-1$ and the offset $\tau j + i$.
As the $i$-th DFT provides the values of $f$ on $W_t + \omega_{\eta j + \tau i}$, if the matrix is in row order in memory, the values are already in the right order for the final return of the values at $\omega_{\eta j + k}$, $k = 0 \ldots \eta - 1$.

### Simplified code

In this prototype, the `dft` function below performs $2^{\text{logstride}}$ DFTs on as many interleaved polynomials (the coefficient $c$ of polynomial number $r$ is at index $c \cdot 2^{\text{logstride}} + r$), which simplifies the recursion and limits the number of function calls performed.

~~~C++
void dft(uint64_t* poly, int s, uint64_t j, unsigned int logstride)
{
    if(s==0)
    {
        uint64_t stride = 1uL << logstride;
        uint64_t local_omega = omega(j << 1);
        for(uint64_t i = 0; i < stride; i++)
        {
            poly[i] = poly[i] ^ multiply(poly[i + stride], local_omega);
            poly[i + stride] = poly[i] ^ poly[i + stride];
            //(u = f_0 + f1* \omega_{2.j}, f_0 + f1 * \omega_{2.j+1} = f_0 + f1 *(\omega_{2.j} + 1) = u + f_1)
        }
    }
    else
    {
        unsigned int t = 1uL << (s-1);
        uint64_t tau = 1uL << t;
        decompose_taylor(poly, tau, logstride);
        // step 2: DFTs on the columns (these are 2^t times as many interleaved polynomials)
        dft(poly, s-1, j, logstride + t);
        // step 3: DFTs on the rows; row i starts at poly + (i * tau << logstride)
        for(uint64_t i = 0; i < tau; i++)
        {
            dft(poly + ((i * tau) << logstride), s-1, (j<<t)|i, logstride);
        }
    }
}
~~~

The main implementation difficulty is to ensure simultaneously a good performance for the multiplication in $\mathbb{F}$ (function `multiply`) and for the evaluation of $j\mapsto \omega_j$ (function `omega`). Computing directly in basis $\beta$ can be tempting (`omega` reduces to the identity in this case), but prevents a very efficient implementation of the product; furthermore, uses of the DFT may require to use a specific basis.

`decompose_taylor` is described in the next section (see also [1]). Interestingly, it can be implemented recursively or iteratively, and for a similar implementation effort, the recursive implementation exhibits better memory locality and is faster (in addition to being much simpler).

### Taylor decomposition

The decomposition of step 1 of the algorithm is described first mathematically, then as it is implemented.

#### Mathematical description

Let $\tau = 2^t$ and $Y = X^\tau + X$. Let $n$ be a power of 2 with $n > \tau$, and $f$ a polynomial of degree $<n$. (In the DFT, $n = \eta = 2^{2t} = \tau^2$, or a smaller power of 2 greater than $\tau$ if only a truncated DFT is computed.) We want the polynomials $f_i$, $i < n/\tau$, such that
$$f = \sum_{i < n/\tau} f_i(X) \, Y^i, \qquad \deg f_i < \tau.$$
Such a decomposition exists and is unique: it is obtained by repeated Euclidean division by $Y$, which has degree $\tau$ (equivalently, the $X^j Y^i$, $j<\tau$, $i<n/\tau$, form a basis of the polynomials of degree $<n$).

**Key identity.** Let $m = n/2$ and $\delta = m/\tau$ (a power of 2, $\delta \geq 1$). Since $\delta$ is a power of 2, the Frobenius map gives
$$Y^\delta = (X^\tau + X)^\delta = X^{\tau\delta} + X^\delta = X^m + X^\delta,$$
i.e. $X^m = Y^\delta + X^\delta$.

**Splitting $f$ in two polynomials of half the size.** Write $f = f_{hi} X^m + f_c$, where $f_c$ consists of the $m$ lower coefficients of $f$, and $f_{hi} = f_a X^{m-\delta} + f_b$, where $f_a$ consists of the $\delta$ highest coefficients of $f$ ($\deg f_a < \delta$, $\deg f_b < m - \delta$). Replacing $X^m$ by $Y^\delta + X^\delta$ in $f_{hi} X^m$, and then in the term $f_a X^m$ that appears in $f_{hi} X^\delta = f_a X^m + f_b X^\delta$, gives

$$f = u \, Y^\delta + v, \qquad u = f_a X^{m-\delta} + f_b + f_a, \qquad v = (f_a + f_b) X^\delta + f_c.$$

$u$ and $v$ have degree $<m$, because $\tau \geq 2$ implies $2\delta \leq m$.

**Recursion.** $u$ and $v$ have degree $< m = \tau\delta$ and are decomposed in the same way, independently: $v = \sum_{i<\delta} v_i Y^i$ and $u = \sum_{i<\delta} u_i Y^i$ with $\deg u_i, \deg v_i < \tau$. Then
$$f = \sum_{i<\delta} v_i Y^i + \sum_{i<\delta} u_i Y^{i+\delta},$$
which is the decomposition of $f$: the $f_i$ are the $v_i$ for $i < \delta$ and the $u_{i-\delta}$ for $i \geq \delta$. The recursion stops when the polynomials have degree $<\tau$ (nothing to do: $f = f_0$). It has $\log_2(n/\tau)$ levels.

**Inverse.** The map $f \mapsto (f_i)_i$ is linear and invertible (it is a change of basis). The inverse is computed by the same recursion in reverse order: recompose $u$ and $v$ from their decompositions, then $f = u\,Y^\delta + v$ by inverting the formulas above ($f_a$, $f_b$ and $f_c$ are recovered from $u$ and $v$ by additions: $f_a$ consists of the $\delta$ highest coefficients of $u$, $f_b$ is the sum of $f_a$ and of the $m-\delta$ lowest coefficients of $u$, and $f_c = v + (f_a + f_b) X^\delta$).

**Example**, for $\tau = 2$, $n=4$, $\delta = 1$: $f = c_0 + c_1 X + c_2 X^2 + c_3 X^3$, $f_a = c_3$, $f_b = c_2$, $f_c = c_0 + c_1 X$, so $u = c_3 X + c_2 + c_3$ and $v = (c_2 + c_3) X + c_0 + c_1 X$. One checks that $f = v + u\,(X^2 + X)$.

#### Implementation

The decomposition is computed in place, on the array of the coefficients of $f$, with additions (XORs) only. The coefficients of $f_i$ replace those of degree $i\tau, \ldots, (i+1)\tau - 1$ of $f$.

**One step.** The formulas for $u$ and $v$ are applied by a single loop of $m$ additions, which writes $v$ in the lower half of the array and $u$ in the upper half (position $m + j$ holds the coefficient of degree $j$ of $u$):

~~~C++
for(uint64_t i = n - 1; i >= m; i--) p[i - m + delta] ^= p[i];
~~~

The loop adds $f_{hi} X^\delta$ to the array, the coefficient at position $m + j$ being added to position $j + \delta$: the terms of degree $<m$ are added to the lower half, and the terms of degree $\geq m$, which come from $f_a$, are added at positions $m, \ldots, m+\delta-1$ of the upper half. It must run in *decreasing* order of $i$. Indeed, for $i = n - \delta + j$, $j < \delta$ (the coefficients of $f_a$), the addition goes to position $m + j$, i.e. in the upper half, where it adds $f_a$ to $f_b$ as needed for $u$. Position $m + j$ is read again at a later iteration of the loop, where its (modified) value is added to position $j + \delta$ of the lower half. This is what adds $(f_a + f_b) X^\delta$ to $f_c$, and not just $f_b X^\delta$, to obtain $v$. For the example above, the loop does $c_2 \leftarrow c_2 + c_3$ then $c_1 \leftarrow c_1 + c_2 + c_3$ (with the new $c_2$), which gives the array $[c_0,\ c_1 + c_2 + c_3,\ c_2 + c_3,\ c_3]$: $v = c_0 + (c_1 + c_2 + c_3) X$ in the lower half, and $u = (c_2 + c_3) + c_3 X$ in the upper half.

**Recursion.** The step is applied recursively to the two halves of the array (the lower half contains $v$, the upper half contains $u$), as long as their size is larger than $\tau$. The coefficients of $u_i$ are then at positions $m + i\tau$, which are the positions of $f_{i+\delta}$.

**Cost.** Each level costs $n/2$ additions, for a total of $\frac{n}{2}\log_2(n/\tau)$ additions and no multiplication. For $n = \tau^2$, as in the DFT, this is $\frac{n}{2} t$.

**Reverse.** The inverse decomposition (used by the inverse DFT) first recomposes the two halves recursively, then performs the same additions as the loop above, but in *increasing* order of $i$: each addition cancels itself, so performing them in the reverse order undoes the loop.

**Interleaved polynomials.** To decompose $2^{\text{logstride}}$ interleaved polynomials at once (coefficient $c$ of polynomial $r$ at index $c \cdot 2^{\text{logstride}} + r$), the same loops are used with all offsets multiplied by $2^{\text{logstride}}$: they then add blocks of consecutive words, which can be vectorized.

**Variants.** The implementation used by the fast product (`mg_decompose_taylor_recursive` in `include/mg_templates.h`) assumes that $n$ is a power of 2 (the polynomial is padded with zeros if needed). The generic version (`decompose_taylor_recursive` in `include/decompose_taylor.h`) accepts any number of coefficients: $n$ is then the smallest power of 2 not below it, the loop only runs over the existing coefficients, and the recursion is on the $m$ low coefficients and on the remaining high ones. Iterative versions (`decompose_taylor_iterative*`) perform the same additions level by level instead of recursively.

An experiment on merging two levels of the recursion into a single step, to reduce the number of passes on the array, and the possible improvements, are described in [taylor_merge.md](taylor_merge.md).

### Remark: products of polynomials, and the last stages of the DFT

In the DFT-based product of two polynomials $f$ and $h$ (see next section), the DFTs of $f$ and $h$ are computed with the same parameters, multiplied pointwise, and an inverse DFT is performed. Consider the last stage (step 3) of the algorithm above. After step 2, row $i$ of the matrix is $f \bmod R_i$ (resp. $h \bmod R_i$), a polynomial of degree $<\tau$. The three operations that follow on this row (DFT of both operands, pointwise product, inverse DFT) amount to computing the product
$$(f \bmod R_i) \, (h \bmod R_i) \bmod R_i, \qquad R_i = X^\tau + X + \omega_{\tau j + i},$$
which is a product in the ring $\mathbb{F}[X]/(R_i)$, and which can also be computed directly: a product of polynomials of degree $<\tau$, followed by a reduction using $X^\tau = X + \omega_{\tau j + i}$ (which is cheap). For small $\tau$ this direct computation is faster than the three DFTs. The "fused variant" of the polynomial product described in the README does exactly this.

### Use of a DFT to perform binary polynomial product

In the binary field of size $2^n$, a common basis is generated by a primitive element $\alpha$. If $\mu$ is the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$ over $\mathbb{F}_2$, the product of two elements of $\mathbb{F}$ in basis $\alpha$ is the product of polynomials over $\mathbb{F}_2$ followed by a modular reduction by $\mu$. As a consequence, given two elements represented by polynomials of degree $< n/2$, their product has degree $< n$ and coincides with the regular polynomial product. This observation can be leveraged as follows to multiply binary polynomials $p$ and $q$ of arbitrary degree:

1) Split $p$ and $q$ into blocks of $n/2$ bits, and map these blocks to coefficients in $\mathbb{F}$, forming two polynomials $\tilde{p}$ and $\tilde{q}$ over $\mathbb{F}$.
2) Compute $\tilde{r} = \tilde{p} \times \tilde{q}$ in $\mathbb{F}[X]$.
3) Coefficient $i$ of $\tilde{r}$ corresponds to coefficients of degree $i \cdot n/2 \ldots i \cdot n/2 + n - 1$ of $r = p \times q$ (these coefficients overlap and the overlapping parts must be added together).

The product computation at step 2 can be performed by two DFTs, the pointwise product of the results, then an inverse DFT. The DFTs must have enough values to determine $\tilde{r}$: their size must be at least $\deg \tilde{r} + 1$.

Let us detail the correspondence between polynomial products in $\mathbb{F}_2[X]$ and in $\mathbb{F}[X]$. Let $\psi: \mathbb{F}_2[X] \to \mathbb{F}$ which associates $\sum_i u_i \alpha^i$ to $\sum_i u_i X^i$. $\psi$ is bijective when restricted to polynomials of degree $ < n$, $\psi^{-1}$ is the inverse of this restricted map.

A polynomial $p=\sum_i p_i X^i$ is written in "blocks"
$$p=\sum_j c_{p,j} \, X^{j \cdot n/2}\ \text{ with }\ \forall j\,,\ c_{p,j} = \sum_{j \cdot n/2 \leq i < (j+1)n/2} p_i X^{i-j \cdot n/2}$$

Then
$$p\, q = \sum_j \sum_{k+\ell=j} c_{p,k} c_{q,\ell} \, X^{j \cdot n/2} = \sum_j \psi^{-1}\left(\sum_{k+\ell=j} \psi(c_{p,k}) \psi(c_{q,\ell}) \right) \, X^{j \cdot n/2}$$

(because each product $c_{p,k} c_{q,\ell}$ has degree $<n$, so that $\psi(c_{p,k} c_{q,\ell}) = \psi(c_{p,k}) \psi(c_{q,\ell})$).

Write $u_j = \sum_{k+\ell=j} \psi(c_{p,k}) \psi(c_{q,\ell})$. Then $p\, q = \sum_j \psi^{-1}(u_j) X^{j \cdot n/2}$.

Note that the polynomials $\psi^{-1}(u_j)$ are in general of degree up to $n-2$, hence they overlap in the sum above: the block $j$ covers the degrees $j \cdot n/2 \ldots j \cdot n/2 + n - 2$, and the next block starts at degree $(j+1) \cdot n/2$.

Compare the product above to the product in $\mathbb{F}[X]$ of $\tilde{p} = \sum_k \psi(c_{p,k}) X^k$ and $\tilde{q} = \sum_{\ell} \psi(c_{q,\ell}) X^{\ell}$:
$$\tilde{p}\, \tilde{q} = \sum_{j} \left(\sum_{k+\ell=j} \psi(c_{p,k}) \psi(c_{q,\ell}) \right) X^j = \sum_j u_j X^j.$$

Therefore this product produces exactly the coefficients needed to compute $p\, q$.

We can derive from this the following method to compute $p \, q$: form the polynomials $\tilde{p}$ and $\tilde{q}$ by forming the coefficients $c_{p,j}$ and $c_{q,j}$ and, considering them as elements of $\mathbb{F}$ (formally, by computing $\psi(c_{p,j})$ and $\psi(c_{q,j})$), compute the product $\tilde{p}\, \tilde{q}$. Then use the coefficients $u_j$ of the result, with the proper overlapping, to compute $p\, q$.

As a final remark, note that this algorithm requires to work with the basis $A = \{ 1, \alpha, \alpha^2,\ldots, \alpha^{n-1} \} $, which is distinct from the Cantor basis used for efficient DFT computation (the evaluation points $\omega_j$ of the DFT, which are defined in the Cantor basis, must be expressed in the basis $A$: this is the role of the function `omega` above). Another incentive to use the basis $A$ is that, with a careful selection of the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$, it enables an efficient implementation of multiplication in $\mathbb{F}$.
